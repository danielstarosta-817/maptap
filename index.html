the geography, the timing, and the odd fact

Round 5 is the assassin

mean accuracy by round position

MapTap sequences each day easy → hard, and it is not subtle: the global field drops 20 points from round 1 to round 5. It also pays them differently. Fitting all 920 posted finals against their round accuracies solves exactly — the scoring is 1×R1 + 1×R2 + 2×R3 + 3×R4 + 3×R5, out of 1000 — so rounds 4 and 5 carry 60% of the day between them and round 1 is worth a tenth of it. The hardest rounds are also the dearest, which is why the whole competition is decided in the last two.

    Does going late help?

    909 scores, each on the poster's own clock

    The theory in the chat is that whoever plays after dinner — or at least not at 7am before coffee — has the edge. The honest answer is that the data can barely test it, because almost nobody plays late. Of 909 scores posted on the puzzle's own day, 654 land between 4am and 9am on the poster's own clock and only 41 come after 2pm. Within that range there is no signal at all: the correlation between local posting time and score is −0.005.

    Gabe and Luke are on Pacific time, Zach on Central, everyone else Eastern. The export came off an Eastern phone, so every timestamp below has been shifted to the poster's own clock — which makes Gabe the earliest riser in the group, not the latest: his median tap is 06:18 his time, and 98% of his scores are in before 9am. It also matches what the chat already knew, Gabe having once clocked Luke “tapping maps at 555 local time”.

    The two columns disagree on purpose, and the gap is the whole lesson. Win rate is confounded by who plays when — the midnight–4am row has the group's best win rate at 29.6%, but 23 of its 27 posts are Brian and Gabe, the two strongest players. The score column subtracts each player's own average first, and once you do that the same row is flat at 0.0. Time of day explains nothing; who is typing explains everything. The 2–6pm and 6pm–midnight rows hold 26 and 15 posts, so treat them as anecdotes rather than evidence.

    The table splits each player's own scores at their own median local posting time, so it compares them only against themselves. Only Zach is better late, by 4.3 points. The obvious caveat throughout: a timestamp records when a score was posted, not when it was played.
